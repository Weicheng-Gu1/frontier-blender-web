## From seeing a scene to rebuilding it

What would it take to reconstruct a building from photographs, a floor plan, and a short brief? A model would have to connect what it sees to what the drawings imply: the depth of a room, the alignment of a staircase, the relationship between a facade and the spaces behind it. Then it would have to make those relationships real in three dimensions.

Multimodal coding agents bring a new possibility to this problem. They can read images and technical drawings, draw on prior knowledge, write Blender programs, and inspect their own renders. Instead of relying on dense photographic coverage alone, they can combine complementary evidence to construct an editable scene. The ambition is reconstruction that benefits from both geometric observation and semantic understanding, even at the scale of buildings and cities.

**AgentRecBench asks how far today's frontier agents can go.** Each task supplies multi-view images with camera parameters, a production specification, and technical drawings. The agent must reconstruct the reference faithfully in Blender. Across 80 tasks, we test whether a plausible overall impression survives closer inspection of geometry, appearance, and individual components.

## Spatial intelligence in action

Reconstruction brings several spatial abilities into one executable task. A useful reference is <a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2512.20617v1">SpatialTree (Xiao et al., 2025, §3)</a>, which organizes spatial abilities into four levels:

<div className="spatial-levels">
<div><span>Perception</span><p>Identify geometry, orientation, location, and relations in the observations.</p></div>
<div><span>Mental mapping</span><p>Connect viewpoints and semantic cues into a coherent representation of space.</p></div>
<div><span>Mental simulation</span><p>Reason about spatial consequences and plan a sequence of operations.</p></div>
<div><span>Agentic competence</span><p>Act through tools, use feedback, and sustain a long sequence of spatial decisions.</p></div>
</div>

In this framework, a Blender reconstruction exercises all four. The agent must infer a scene, implement it, render it, and use the discrepancies to decide what to fix next. The resulting artifact makes its decisions inspectable: a door is in a particular wall; a controller has a particular thickness; a street connects particular blocks. An error can originate in perception, inference, planning, or execution, and the reconstructed scene gives us a concrete place to investigate.

## Expert annotation makes detail measurable

The benchmark spans **16 objects, 11 object arrangements, 23 interiors, 13 buildings, 12 blocks, and 5 cities**. Every task combines three forms of evidence:

<div className="input-triptych">
<div><span>Appearance and viewpoints</span><h3>Multi-view images</h3><p>Images and camera parameters tie visual observations to a shared 3D coordinate system.</p></div>
<div><span>The reconstruction brief</span><h3>Production specification</h3><p>A client-style description identifies the essential structures and details that must survive delivery.</p></div>
<div><span>Spatial constraints</span><h3>Technical drawings</h3><p>Plans, elevations, and dimensioned views clarify structure that appearance alone may leave ambiguous.</p></div>
</div>

The collection includes **994 input images and 616 drawing sheets**. Tasks provide between 3 and 50 images, depending on the scene. Agents choose their own modeling process within **21,600 seconds** and submit an editable Blender scene.

Expert annotation connects the full scene to its semantic parts. It defines **2,155 semantic regions and 8 whole-scene targets**, so evaluation can ask whether a room's contents are reconstructed as well as its enclosing shell. These regions are fixed in the ground-truth world coordinate system. A component moved to the wrong place cannot be rescued by aligning it after submission.

<div className="evaluation-flow"><div><strong>Reference evidence</strong><span>Images · specification · drawings</span></div><div><strong>Agent reconstruction</strong><span>Code · Blender · inspection</span></div><div><strong>Fixed-reference evaluation</strong><span>695 held-out views · 2,163 surface targets</span></div></div>

Evaluation combines appearance with spatial agreement. PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS measure complementary aspects of rendered appearance; depth tests surfaces along the camera rays; bidirectional point-to-surface Chamfer distance tests geometric fidelity. The combined score weights these metrics at **5%, 10%, 17.5%, 17.5%, and 50%**, respectively. Shared cameras and fixed semantic regions keep the comparison anchored to the reference.

## When the scene grows, the details slip

<Wide><div className="blog-wide"><FidelityExplorer/></div></Wide>

Across the eight configurations, category-average scores are **64.0 for objects, 60.2 for arrangements, 32.2 for interiors, 28.1 for buildings, 22.2 for blocks, and 21.2 for cities**. The sharpest drop appears between object arrangements and interiors: agents must now coordinate an enclosing space, repeated structures, furniture, and local details.

The images let you inspect how that transition looks for each model. Category averages summarize different task sets; the averages also reflect which tasks belong to each category.

## The room is there. Its components are not yet faithful.

A room can look structurally plausible while its furniture and fixtures remain inaccurate. We compare room-level and component-level surface scores on **27 matched tasks, covering 551 components**.

<ComponentAnalysis/>

For GPT-6-Astra (high), the mean room-level score is **44.65**, while the component-level score is **14.12**. Across configurations, room-level fidelity exceeds component-level fidelity on 23–26 of the 27 tasks. Capturing the larger envelope does not guarantee that the smaller structures have the right geometry or position.

For Astra (high), the mean task-level fraction of components scoring below 1 is **24.7%**; the fraction with no reconstructed surface is **7.3%**. These measure different failures. A very low score can reflect a misplaced or poorly shaped component even when some geometry exists.

## Materials, lighting, and the geometry between pixels

The handheld console is immediately recognizable in Kimi's reconstruction. Yet its front-view PSNR is **17.27 dB**. To understand the discrepancy, we replace selected properties with their ground-truth counterparts while keeping the camera and evaluation mask fixed.

<Wide><div className="blog-wide diagnostic-wide"><DiagnosticExplorer/></div></Wide>

Replacing materials and lighting raises front-view PSNR to **24.06 dB**, reducing foreground pixel MSE by **79.1% without changing geometry**. Appearance explains much of this view's error, but it does not resolve the side view: after the same appearance intervention, side-view PSNR is only **12.79 dB**. Replacing the button structures raises it to **20.62 dB**; replacing the controller geometry as well raises it to **21.37 dB**.

The causal distinction matters. Color and illumination dominate one view, while local structure dominates another. Recognizing a familiar object is easier than reproducing the surfaces that generate each pixel.

## A strong appearance score can hide a depth error

The space-station reconstruction by GPT-6-Astra (low) scores **98.56 in LPIPS** and **83.18 in surface fidelity**, but only **16.33 in depth**. A broad view can preserve the station's identity while a grazing view exposes displaced panels and incorrect occlusion.

On a grazing held-out view, depth MAE reaches **9.41 m** even as the normalized LPIPS score remains **99.45**. This is why evaluation checks both images and geometry, across multiple fixed viewpoints and semantic targets.

## Toward faithful reconstruction

Frontier agents can already turn heterogeneous references into recognizable, editable scenes. The harder step is preserving local structure as those scenes become more complex. AgentRecBench makes that distinction measurable: the scene's envelope, its components, its appearance, and its spatial arrangement can each be examined against the same reference.

Progress here means more than a better-looking render. It means agents that can use drawings, images, and specifications together, identify what their reconstruction still gets wrong, and refine the scene until its details agree.

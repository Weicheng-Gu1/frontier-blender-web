### From Seeing a World to Building One

What would it take to reconstruct a 3D scene from multi-view images with camera parameters, technical drawings, and a production specification? An agent has to connect what appears in the views to what the drawings constrain—room depth, staircase alignment, hidden partitions, and how a facade relates to the spaces behind it. Then it must turn those relationships into a complete, editable Blender scene anchored in a shared world coordinate system.

Coding with Blender is no longer new on its own—agents already generate shapes and edit scenes. What makes spatial reconstruction a decisive test of intelligence is synthesizing heterogeneous visual and dimensional evidence, writing Blender code, rendering its own work, spotting structural flaws—such as a wall beside stairs that is too open or a partition blocking cabinetry—and revising the scene itself. Instead of relying on dense visual capture alone, the agent reconciles plans, elevations, sections, and views to build geometry that holds together.

AgentRecBench asks how far today's frontier agents can go. Each task supplies multi-view images with camera poses and intrinsics, production specifications, and technical drawings, requiring the agent to reconstruct the reference faithfully in Blender. Across 80 tasks, we test whether a plausible first impression survives rigorous checks on geometry, appearance, and individual components in shared world coordinates.

The agent receives 23 multi-view images with camera parameters, 13 technical drawings, and a production specification for a two-storey villa. Its deliverable is a single editable Blender scene containing the house, pool court, detached pavilion, and furnishings.

<div className="article-section" id="evaluation"><span className="sr-only">Evaluation</span></div>

Evaluation compares each submission with an expert-built Blender scene in a shared world coordinate system. Across the benchmark, 695 held-out views test appearance and depth, and 2,163 fixed targets test surface geometry. The overall score combines PSNR (5%), SSIM (10%), LPIPS (17.5%), depth (17.5%), and bidirectional surface distance (50%).

### Reconstruction Fidelity Degrades with Scene Complexity

<CategoryFidelity/>

Across all 10 configurations, reconstruction fidelity drops steadily at every step up in scene scale and structural density—falling from **65.0** on **Objects** and **60.5** on **Multi-Object Arrangements**, down to **32.3** on **Interiors**, **28.1** on **Buildings**, **22.0** on **Urban & Campus Blocks**, and **21.0** on **City-Scale Environments**.

The sharpest break occurs between **Multi-Object Arrangements (60.5)** and **Interiors (32.3)**. Arranging a few standalone pieces on a tea table is still within reach; enclosing those objects inside walls, ceilings, staircases, and multi-room layouts nearly cuts accuracy in half. Beyond interiors, every added layer of architectural coupling—connecting rooms into full buildings, blocks, and city districts—compounds geometric and occlusion errors, driving fidelity down toward a **21.0** floor.

### Global Structure Does Not Guarantee Local Fidelity

A room can look structurally plausible while its furniture and fixtures remain inaccurate. We compare room-level and component-level surface scores on **27 matched tasks, covering 551 components**.

<ComponentAnalysis/>

For **GPT-6.2-Astra (high)**, the room-level surface score averages **61.28/100**, compared with **24.72/100** for individual components. The room scores higher in **22 of the 27 tasks**. Averaging the component fractions separately within each task, **24.9%** of targets score below 1/100 and **11.8%** contain no predicted surface at all. The library coat rack scores 10.99/100 with the wrong hook shape and arrangement. The kitchen bin, placed outside its reference box, scores zero.

### Visual Plausibility Can Conceal Geometric Errors

The handheld console is immediately recognizable in Kimi's reconstruction. Yet its front-view PSNR is **17.27 dB**. To understand the discrepancy, we replace selected properties with their ground-truth counterparts while keeping the camera and evaluation mask fixed.

<Wide><div className="blog-wide diagnostic-wide"><DiagnosticExplorer/></div></Wide>

Replacing materials and lighting raises front-view PSNR to **24.06 dB**, reducing foreground pixel MSE by **79.1% without changing geometry**. Appearance explains much of this view's error, but it does not resolve the side view: after the same appearance intervention, side-view PSNR is only **12.79 dB**. Replacing the button structures raises it to **20.62 dB**; replacing the controller geometry as well raises it to **21.37 dB**.

#### How much can the image change without moving a vertex?

We rendered the same Kimi console under ten settings at all four hidden cameras. For each material edit we changed one Principled BSDF input and kept the lights fixed. The colored controller shells start at roughness 0.34 (blue) and 0.35 (red). Lowering roughness to 0.08 concentrates the specular highlight. Raising it to 0.85 spreads the reflected light over a wider range of directions. A separate edit raises metallic from 0 to 0.80, changing the balance between diffuse and tinted specular reflection. We also polished the display glass (0.72 → 0.10) and roughened the graphite housing (0.50 → 0.85). [Blender’s material parameter definitions](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/4.5/render/shader_nodes/shader/principled.html).

For the lighting edits we restored every original material and changed only the key light. The original rig uses a 1,000 W area key, a 500 W fill and a 1,000 W rim. We halved the area key’s power, then tested point, spot and sun keys separately with the fill, rim and world fixed. The point and spot use a 0.15 m radius, and the spot has a 65° cone. The sun uses an irradiance of 3 W/m² and an angular diameter of 4°. The sun and local lights have different physical units, so these comparisons describe the specified rigs at their stated settings.

<Wide><div className="blog-wide diagnostic-wide"><AppearanceStudy/></div></Wide>

**Material.** Polishing the display raised four-view mean PSNR from **15.54 to 16.29 dB**. Giving the controller shells a metallic finish lowered it to **14.72 dB**. Across those same two settings, raw LPIPS moved only from **0.1709 to 0.1768** (lower is better). Changing shell roughness alone had a smaller effect: the glossy and matte finishes scored **15.51** and **15.47 dB**. Which material changes matters as much as the size of the parameter change.

**Lighting.** On the front view, the original area key scored **17.27 dB**. Halving its power raised PSNR to **18.38 dB**; the point and spot keys each reached **18.54 dB**, and the sun key reached **17.81 dB**. The point key reduced front-view foreground MSE by roughly a quarter, yet raw LPIPS shifted only from **0.0714 to 0.0629**. The same change *lowered* underside-view PSNR from **11.78 to 11.40 dB**. Relighting can improve one photograph while making another less faithful.

**Geometry.** All forty depth maps were pixel-identical to their respective camera baselines. Mean depth therefore stayed at **83.91/100**, and the fixed mesh retained its measured surface score of **86.23/100**. Across the ten settings, four-view normalized LPIPS varied by just **0.31 points**. These results distinguish three questions: whether reflected light lands at the right pixels, whether the object still looks perceptually similar, and whether its surfaces occupy the right places. The earlier button replacement shows the converse: a geometric correction can explain a large view-specific error that relighting leaves behind.

The space-station reconstruction by GPT-6-Astra (low) scores **98.56 in LPIPS** and **83.18 in surface fidelity**, but only **16.33 in depth**. A broad view can preserve the station's identity while a grazing view exposes displaced panels and incorrect occlusion.

<div className="article-section" id="resources"><span className="sr-only">Conclusion &amp; Resources</span></div>

Frontier agents can already turn heterogeneous references into recognizable, editable scenes. The harder step is preserving local structure as those scenes become more complex. AgentRecBench makes that distinction measurable: the scene's envelope, its components, its appearance, and its spatial arrangement can each be examined against the same reference.
